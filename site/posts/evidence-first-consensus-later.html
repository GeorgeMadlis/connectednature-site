Fifth Inquiry

Evidence First, Consensus Later

This article asks whether Earth-system digital twins, AI agents, and blockchain-based provenance records can make environmental disagreement more inspectable — not by resolving it, but by documenting it with enough rigour that its structure becomes visible, stable, and contestable.

Originating statement

“Earth-related digital twins cannot automatically solve environmental conflicts, but they can make those conflicts more inspectable by linking claims to data, models, assumptions, uncertainty, provenance, and governance decisions.”

The statement is modest where it needs to be and ambitious where it can afford to be. It rejects the idea that a sufficiently sophisticated simulation of Earth systems can determine how those systems should be governed. It claims instead that better-organised evidence — evidence with documented provenance, explicit assumptions, versioned model states, and structured uncertainty — can raise the quality of environmental disagreement even when it cannot settle it.

This post continues a sequence of inquiries that began with the question of whether ecological limits can be made economically binding through transaction-level mechanisms, and developed in the preceding article into the principle that evidence infrastructure must be built before financial instruments, not after. The present inquiry extends that logic from financial instruments to environmental governance more broadly. Evidence must come before automated recommendations, DAO decisions, AI-generated summaries, and claims to scientific consensus for the same structural reasons it must come before tokenisation: downstream authority cannot confer upstream credibility.

Why this matters

The phrase “Earth digital twin” has entered policy language quickly. It now appears in discussions of climate projection, biodiversity monitoring, land-use change, ocean observation, and smart-city planning. The European Commission’s Destination Earth initiative proposes a high-resolution digital replica of the Earth system capable of supporting environmental policy at scale. Similar proposals are being developed by technology companies, national agencies, and international scientific organisations.

The ambition is understandable. If a digital twin can represent the coupled dynamics of climate, hydrology, land cover, ecosystem function, and human activity, it might be able to show what follows from different governance choices: which intervention preserves more biodiversity, which emission pathway stays within a temperature threshold, which land-use transition reduces flood risk while sustaining agricultural output.

But the history of environmental policy suggests that the problem is rarely ignorance of consequences. More often it is disagreement about which consequences matter, to whom, over what time horizon, under whose authority, and at whose cost. A digital twin that produces more precise projections does not dissolve that disagreement. If poorly designed, it may give one set of interests a more technically imposing platform from which to override others.

The question is not whether Earth digital twins are useful. It is whether they are being designed with a realistic account of what they can and cannot do when deployed inside political conflicts about nature, land, climate, and resources.

Research question

Can Earth-system digital twins, evidence bundles, blockchain-based provenance records, and AI agents provide a practical infrastructure for documenting environmental conflicts more rigorously, without claiming to resolve them automatically — and if so, what design principles distinguish a system that makes disagreement inspectable from one that merely amplifies the authority of whoever controls the model?

Why Earth digital twins differ from their engineering counterparts

The digital twin concept originates in engineering and manufacturing. A digital twin of a jet engine or wind turbine is a continuously updated computational model of a specific physical object, linked in real time to sensor data from that object. Its purpose is to monitor state, predict failure modes, and optimise performance within well-defined operational parameters. The boundaries of the twin are the boundaries of the object. The objectives — safety, efficiency, longevity — are largely agreed in advance.

Earth-system digital twins work differently in almost every respect. They do not represent a single bounded object. They assemble partial, contested, multi-scale evidence about forests, rivers, soils, atmosphere, ocean, biodiversity, land use, infrastructure, and human activity. Their data sources are inconsistent, unevenly distributed across the planet, and governed by different legal and scientific norms. Their models embed assumptions about system boundaries, feedback mechanisms, aggregation methods, and scenario parameters that are themselves subjects of scientific disagreement.

Most consequentially, the objectives of an Earth digital twin are not agreed. A twin deployed to support forest conservation policy must represent biodiversity, carbon storage, watershed function, and rural livelihoods simultaneously. Each of these dimensions is measured differently, weighted differently by different stakeholders, and subject to different legal frameworks. There is no operational parameter analogous to “the engine must not overheat.” The system represents competing claims about what nature is worth and who gets to decide.

This difference is not a limitation to be overcome by better technology. It is a structural feature of the domain. A design that ignores it will not produce a neutral decision tool. It will produce an evidence architecture whose political choices are hidden inside modelling parameters.

From evidence bundles to conflict bundles

The preceding post in this series introduced the concept of evidence bundles in the context of EUDR compliance and nature-backed currency: a deterministic, version-controlled, publicly inspectable record of the data inputs, model parameters, and verification decisions that support a specific ecological claim. An evidence bundle gives a currency instrument or compliance certificate something concrete to reference — not just an assertion that a forest exists and is healthy, but a documented record of how that conclusion was reached, when, by whom, and using which methods.

Environmental conflicts require an extension of that concept. When multiple parties make competing claims about the same forest, river, or land-use decision, the relevant unit is not a single evidence bundle but what might be called a conflict bundle: a structured record of the competing claims, the evidence each references, the models and assumptions each relies upon, and the dimensions along which the disagreement runs.

A conflict bundle for a forest management dispute might document that one party’s claim rests on near-term carbon accounting over a twenty-year horizon using one model family, while another party’s claim rests on timber-substitution effects over a fifty-year horizon using a different model family. The disagreement may not be about the underlying data at all. It may be entirely about temporal boundaries and accounting conventions. Making that explicit is itself a significant contribution to the quality of the dispute.

A conflict bundle also distinguishes the different dimensions of disagreement. Some disputes are empirical: the parties disagree about what the data show. Some are model disputes: the parties share data but apply different analytical frameworks. Some are legal: one interpretation is permitted under applicable law and another is not, regardless of what the models say. Some are value disputes: the parties assign different weights to different outcomes, and no further evidence will resolve that difference. Collapsing these dimensions into a single output — a dashboard score, a recommendation, a compliance verdict — destroys exactly the information that governance needs.

Blockchain as provenance, not truth

The preceding post in this series argued that blockchain anchoring provides provenance, not scientific authority. That principle applies here with equal force, and in a context where it is easier to misapply.

In environmental governance, a blockchain record can usefully document when a claim was submitted, which evidence bundle it referenced, which model version was used, which review decision was recorded, and whether a claim was subsequently updated, challenged, or deprecated. This is valuable. It makes the history of a conflict inspectable and resistant to unilateral revision. It creates an audit trail that external parties can follow without relying on the goodwill of any single institution.

What a blockchain record cannot do is determine whether a biodiversity model is scientifically valid, whether a forest policy is ethically justified, whether a climate attribution claim deserves equal weight with a competing claim, or whether an economic trade-off is socially acceptable. The immutability of the record preserves the history of assertions. It does not certify the quality of the reasoning behind them.

This distinction matters practically because blockchain-based environmental governance proposals sometimes conflate the two. An on-chain record of a measurement is not equivalent to a peer-reviewed finding. A DAO vote on a model parameter is not equivalent to scientific consensus. A tokenised credit anchored to a satellite data feed is not equivalent to a verified carbon removal claim. Making the provenance trail inspectable is necessary but not sufficient. The quality of what is being recorded remains a separate question, answered by methods that blockchains do not provide.

AI agents as summarisers of disagreement, not judges of truth

Agentic AI systems are increasingly proposed as coordinators and evaluators within environmental decision pipelines. The argument is practical: the volume of ecological data, scientific literature, regulatory documentation, and stakeholder submissions relevant to a land-use decision or climate policy question now exceeds what human teams can process at the speed that governance requires. An AI agent that can identify relevant evidence, detect inconsistencies, summarise competing positions, and flag gaps can accelerate deliberation without replacing it.

That use case is real. The risk lies in the next step: treating the agent’s summary as a verdict. An agent trained on scientific literature and equipped with retrieval tools may produce outputs with the surface appearance of authoritative synthesis. If those outputs are fed directly into decision pipelines — as inputs to DAO votes, as parameters for automated compliance systems, as the basis for credit issuance — the human judgment that should evaluate the quality of the synthesis has been bypassed.

The appropriate role for agentic AI in environmental conflict documentation is bounded and derivative. An agent can assist with source discovery across repositories and databases. It can screen datasets for completeness, temporal coverage, and consistency with stated scope. It can extract claims from documents and map them to a structured conflict bundle. It can identify when two claims use incompatible definitions of the same term, or when a modelling assumption in one submission contradicts a modelling assumption in another. It can produce plain-language summaries of technical positions for non-specialist audiences.

What it should not do is silently convert a conflict bundle into a recommendation, weight competing claims by their coherence with its training distribution, or treat the absence of a retrieved counterclaim as evidence that none exists. The agent reads and organises the evidence bundle. Its authority is derivative. The bundle is primary.

Example: Estonian forest management

The Estonian clear-cutting dispute offers a useful test case because it is a genuine multi-objective conflict with active legal, scientific, economic, and ecological dimensions rather than a hypothetical arrangement.

Estonia has one of Europe’s highest recent forest-loss rates, and Estonian forest management is predominantly clear-cutting based. BirdLife Europe reports that harvested forest areas in Estonia rose sharply in 2016-2018 compared with 2004-2015, and that clear-cutting accounts for most logging cases. Eurostat’s 2026 forestry update adds a further pressure signal: in 2023, Estonia was the only EU country with available data where wood removals exceeded net annual increment. Environmental organisations, ecologists, and increasingly the European Commission have argued that current rates threaten both biodiversity and the carbon sink function of Estonian forests. The forest industry and rural communities have countered that sustainable forestry supports livelihoods and that timber substitution for fossil-intensive materials produces climate benefits that critics’ accounting frameworks omit. Legal proceedings at national and European level have added institutional complexity: some clear-cutting decisions have been challenged as violations of the Habitats and Birds Directives, producing rulings that shift the legal baseline without settling the underlying scientific dispute.

A conflict bundle for this dispute would record, among other things, the divergent time horizons in the competing carbon accounts; the boundary choices that determine whether substitution effects enter the calculation; the spatial scale at which biodiversity indicators are aggregated; the legal instruments that apply to specific forest types and ownership categories; the economic models used to estimate sectoral employment and export dependence; and the areas of genuine scientific uncertainty, such as the rate at which post-harvest forests recover their carbon storage and their capacity to support specialist species.

An Earth digital twin designed to support this conflict would not produce a single answer to the question of whether Estonia should reduce clear-cutting. It would make visible what the answer would be under each of the competing frameworks: short-term carbon accounting, long-term timber-substitution accounting, biodiversity weighting, rural-economic priority, legal-compliance priority. Governance bodies would then be required to make the trade-off explicit, rather than receiving a model output that has already made it invisible.

This is a meaningful contribution. It does not resolve the conflict. It makes the conflict more honest.

Example: climate-change attribution

The climate attribution case must be handled differently, and it is worth stating why explicitly.

The basic attribution question — whether recent global warming is primarily human-caused — is not an open scientific dispute in the same sense as the Estonian forest question. The evidence base supporting anthropogenic attribution is extremely large, methodologically diverse, independently replicated, and subject to sustained critical scrutiny over several decades. A conflict-documentation system that treated the mainstream scientific position and politically motivated counterclaims as parallel entries in a symmetrical conflict bundle would not be documenting disagreement honestly. It would be producing false equivalence.

The relevant distinction is between scientifically open questions and politically amplified controversies. A well-designed conflict bundle must classify the disagreement before documenting it. Some disputes involve genuine scientific uncertainty: the magnitude of specific feedbacks, the sensitivity of regional precipitation to particular forcing agents, the rate of ice-sheet dynamics under different warming trajectories. These belong inside the conflict bundle as open empirical questions. The basic attribution question does not belong there. Placing it there is a design error, not a commitment to intellectual balance.

This principle — that a conflict-documentation system must distinguish open scientific uncertainty from manufactured controversy — is essential if the system is to be useful rather than weaponisable. An Earth digital twin designed for governance cannot be neutral between questions that the scientific community has answered and questions that remain genuinely open. It must encode that distinction, make it inspectable, and maintain it against political pressure to present settled findings as contested.

What the system should record

A conflict-documentation infrastructure built on Earth digital twin architecture should at minimum record the following for each claim that enters a governance process: what is asserted; who asserts it; which evidence bundle is referenced; which model version was used; which assumptions were made about system boundaries, time horizon, and spatial aggregation; what uncertainty ranges are reported; what counterclaims exist and which evidence they reference; along which dimensions the disagreement runs — empirical, model-based, legal, economic, or value-based; and when each of these records was created or updated, and by whom.

These are not requirements beyond current technical capability. Many of them are already implemented in scientific data infrastructures, regulatory compliance systems, and version-controlled research repositories. The innovation required is not primarily technical. It is institutional: building the governance norms, interoperability standards, and review procedures that allow these records to be maintained with sufficient integrity to be relied upon in adversarial environments.

What the system must not pretend to do

The failure mode is predictable. A system that begins as evidence infrastructure will face pressure to become decision infrastructure. Governance bodies will want outputs they can act on directly. Platform providers will find decision support more commercially valuable than evidence documentation. Funders will want demonstrations of impact. AI agents will be given prompts that ask for recommendations rather than structured summaries.

Each of these pressures is individually understandable and collectively dangerous. When a conflict bundle is compressed into a dashboard score, the weighting choices that determined the score are no longer visible. When an agent’s summary is treated as a finding, the gap in the underlying evidence that the agent did not surface is no longer on the record. When a DAO vote on a model parameter is presented as a scientific determination, the political process by which the parameter was chosen is obscured.

The system must not present model outputs as if they were measurements, compress value-laden trade-offs into dimensionless scores, treat the absence of a retrieved counterclaim as confirmation of a claim, or allow its provenance record to be interpreted as peer review. None of these prohibitions is technically exotic. All of them require explicit design choices and institutional enforcement.

Critical synthesis

The originating statement holds. Earth-system digital twins cannot automatically resolve environmental conflicts, and the claim that they can would be technically false and politically dangerous. But they can do something more modest and more durable: they can build the evidence infrastructure within which environmental disagreement becomes structured, inspectable, and contestable rather than shapeless, opaque, and unreviewable.

The concept of a conflict bundle extends the evidence-bundle logic developed for ecological accounting and EUDR compliance into governance territory. It preserves the same underlying principle: downstream instruments — whether financial, regulatory, or deliberative — must not overwrite the upstream evidence layer. Currency is downstream of evidence. Governance decisions are downstream of evidence. AI-generated recommendations are downstream of evidence. Consensus, where it becomes possible, is downstream of evidence, review, and responsibility.

Three specific findings follow from this inquiry. First, Earth digital twins differ from engineering digital twins in ways that are structural, not contingent: their objectives are contested, their data are heterogeneous, and their outputs carry political weight that cannot be neutralised by technical sophistication. Second, blockchain anchoring provides an integrity record, not a truth record, and that distinction must be maintained in system design and public communication. Third, AI agents can usefully extend human capacity to organise, compare, and summarise complex evidence, but their role in governance pipelines must remain derivative and explicitly bounded, with human review retained at the point where evidence becomes input to a decision.

The claim that digital twins will transform environmental governance is likely correct in a narrow sense: they will change the information environment within which governance operates. Whether that change improves governance depends on design choices that are not yet being made carefully enough. Making those choices well requires treating evidence infrastructure as a prior condition of governance legitimacy, not as a technical support layer added after the governance structure is already decided.

Limits

This article does not evaluate specific Earth digital twin implementations against the design criteria it proposes. It does not model the conditions under which a conflict bundle could be maintained with sufficient integrity to be relied upon in adversarial regulatory environments. It does not examine the distributional effects of evidence-documentation requirements — specifically, whether smaller stakeholders with fewer technical resources would be systematically disadvantaged in a conflict-bundle-based governance system. It does not address the international coordination problem: environmental conflicts frequently cross jurisdictions, and a conflict bundle maintained under one legal system may be inadmissible or inaccessible in another. These remain open tasks.

Open questions

  • Under what institutional conditions can a conflict bundle be maintained with sufficient integrity to be used in adversarial legal or regulatory processes?
  • How should a conflict-documentation system classify the boundary between genuine scientific uncertainty and politically amplified controversy, and who should hold authority over that classification?
  • What governance model prevents an Earth digital twin from being captured by the interests of its most technically capable users?
  • Can conflict-bundle infrastructure be designed so that smaller stakeholders and civil society organisations can participate without requiring the same technical resources as large corporations and state agencies?
  • What is the appropriate relationship between AI agent outputs and formal governance procedures — and where specifically should the human-review requirement be enforced?
  • How should a conflict bundle handle the deprecation of evidence: when data are withdrawn, models are revised, or legal frameworks change, what remains on the record and what is corrected?
