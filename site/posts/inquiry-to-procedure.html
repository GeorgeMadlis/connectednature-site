Sixth Inquiry

From Inquiry to Procedure: Human–AI Research as Progressive Reduction

How recorded research evolution can become deterministic pipelines, better AI instructions, and explicit unresolved questions. This article tests a proposition about method rather than about nature: that the value of recording a human–AI inquiry lies not in preserving every step forever, but in letting an underspecified problem be reduced, part by part, into procedures, instructions, and clearly marked uncertainty.

Originating statement

“In human–AI research, the value of recording an inquiry trajectory lies not in preserving every intermediate step indefinitely, but in enabling an initially underspecified problem to be progressively reduced into deterministic procedures where possible, improved AI instructions where judgement remains necessary, and explicitly retained uncertainty where neither reduction is yet justified.”

The statement is deliberately contestable. It replaces an earlier and weaker formulation in which the recorded trajectory was treated mainly as persistent external memory — valuable simply because it survived. That framing is too static. It gives the log worth for existing rather than for doing work. The claim under test here is stronger and more falsifiable: that the record has methodological value insofar as it helps convert a poorly bounded problem into a more compact and reliable representation.

A defensible negative answer is available, which is what makes the statement worth investigating. The trajectory has little methodological value if the same deterministic procedure, the same agent instructions, and the same unresolved-question set can be derived reliably from the final evidence alone — or if recorded intermediate states mainly propagate obsolete assumptions and scope errors into later work. If that negative holds, the record is provenance, not method.

This test does not start from a blank evidence architecture. The preceding inquiry in this sequence, Evidence First, Consensus Later, argued that Earth-system digital twins, AI agents, blockchain-based provenance mechanisms, and DAO-style governance layers should not silently convert contested environmental claims into authoritative conclusions, and that inspectable evidence and structured disagreement should stand upstream of consensus. It left open a different problem: the evidence architecture through which that inspection and disagreement are supposed to run is not necessarily correct in advance. Categories, bundle boundaries, validation rules, source roles, and agent instructions may initially be wrong. This article investigates how human–AI inquiry can discover, correct, and formalise that architecture — a methodological question with its own scope, not an application of the earlier one.

Why this matters

Both human and AI inquiry are bounded, and they are bounded in ways that compound. Sensors and datasets capture only part of the world. Scientific knowledge is itself incomplete. A given researcher commands only a fraction of the relevant science: someone fluent in geospatial processing may be unfamiliar with a specific regulatory distinction, while someone who knows the law may misjudge remote-sensing uncertainty. An AI system works within the context, tools, and sources it is handed, and within the limits of its own reasoning.

These bounds interact. A human supplies an initial decomposition that carries implicit assumptions, an imperfect breakdown, or ambiguous terminology. The model executes inside that frame and can amplify it rather than question it. The result is systematic scope drift that neither party introduced alone. If this is the common failure mode, then recording an inquiry matters only when the record helps expose and repair the framing — not when it merely stores it. The distinction between a log that documents error and a log that helps correct it is the practical stake of this article.

The consequence for evidence-first governance is a constraint, not a proof. “Evidence first” does not mean that the evidence schema is given first. The architecture used to classify, bundle, validate, compare, and contest evidence is itself a provisional research output, and it can contain framing errors, scope drift, inappropriate bundle boundaries, mistaken source classifications, or invalid decision rules — an explicit evidence schema is not thereby a correct one. It must therefore be corrigible, versionable, and contestable in the same way the claims it structures are. The two bundles show this kind of correction is not confined to fixing data: it includes discovering that a boundary, observer category, or claim class was incorrectly specified — as when the self-referential bundle's own object of study had to be widened at round four, demoting disagreement to one stage among five — and discovering that reproducible, hash-verified provenance had been mistaken for source authenticity, as the fabricated-input episode discussed later shows. Neither was an error in applying a correct schema; each was an error in the schema itself. The implication is specific: before evidence infrastructure can support contestable governance, the machinery used to structure that evidence must itself be open to correction. This does not require that every such correction remain permanently active; the architecture remains provisional where it has not yet survived scrutiny, not indefinitely by default.

Research question

Can recorded human–AI inquiry help progressively transform an initially underspecified research problem into a more reliable computational architecture — reducing some parts to deterministic procedures, translating recurring non-deterministic lessons into improved AI instructions, and preserving only the uncertainty that cannot yet be responsibly reduced?

Two subsidiary questions sharpen it. First: when should accumulated inquiry history cease to be part of an AI's active context and instead be compressed into code, tests, specifications, or agent instructions? Second: can scope errors caused jointly by human framing and AI execution be converted into instructions that prevent the same class of error in later research? Both assume, against the earlier memory framing, that a trajectory need not remain permanently active to have done its work.

Evidence base

The evidence here is not a literature but two recorded human–AI trajectories held under version control. They are published as two self-contained bundles committed into the COIN research folder — each a flattened export carrying its own round ledger, per-round records, and, in the first case, a metrics table and a retrospective classification. The first bundle describes the observer-disagreement framework itself: a general and partly self-referential case in which the inquiry examines its own recording, across twenty rounds contributed by two agents. The second is a public reading snapshot of an applied geospatial bundle for Fazenda Sucuri coffee screening under the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), with generated PDF, JSON, GeoJSON, and report artefacts omitted from COIN. What matters in each is not the number of rounds but the transitions that produced a more general rule, a tool, a specification, a test, or an instruction.

The self-referential bundle records the article's central move directly. At round four, the log states that a human instruction widened the object of study from observer disagreement to statement evolution, demoting disagreement to one stage of a five-stage trajectory; the change is written as a frame transition with content hashes and supersession links on every affected file. The human's own framing was not a fixed input but something the inquiry had to revise, and the revision is inspectable rather than narrated. Later rounds show the two other kinds of reduction: provisional conventions are made normative and then executable as a generator and validator, and — after a foundation audit — the specification is repaired so that every future bundle must disclose its use of Wolfram observer theory as a formal analogy, enforced by validator rejection conditions rather than left to good intentions.

The EUDR bundle offers a public worked example of corrected decomposition inside an applied screening task. Its rounds show review findings becoming concrete repairs: a Sentinel-2 visual defect is fixed, AOI administrative labels and report-map behavior are corrected, the coffee temporal mask is repaired so current coffee preserves the baseline footprint while “new commodity since baseline” remains separate, and a recurring regional overview gap is pushed down into the renderer and publish contract. A further question exposes a level of reduction still available. The regulation defines a deforestation-free condition common across the relevant commodities in terms of whether goods were produced on land not subject to deforestation after 31 December 2020, and Article 3 prohibits placing such commodities on the Union market unless that condition is met. Read this way, the method decomposes more cleanly than a one-piece “coffee mask” allows:

  • Layer A — commodity-independent land evidence. Was the land forest? Did deforestation occur after the cutoff? Which datasets support or contradict this?
  • Layer B — production linkage. Is this the plot or establishment on which the relevant commodity associated with the product was actually produced? EUDR due diligence requires the geolocation of production, so an observation that deforestation occurred somewhere is not sufficient on its own.
  • Layer C — commodity-specific evidence. For coffee, the source-specific commodity evidence matters: FDP-only, MapBiomas-only, and both-source agreement cannot be collapsed into the same warrant merely because they all describe coffee.

This is a good case precisely because it refuses to turn a screening signal into more than the evidence supports. The recorded sequence lets one inspect how a source-specific coffee assumption was corrected, why visual and report defects mattered, and how those findings were converted into more durable method constraints. The correction is visible in the bundle's own artefacts: FDP reports 0.27 hectares of post-2020 baseline-forest loss intersecting new coffee, while MapBiomas and the both-source agreement layer report 0.0 hectares for the same conversion intersection. The bundle keeps those as source disagreement, not as a number to average away. Its current answer remains human-review-required and pinned-not-reproduced, and it states plainly that the result is a screening flag, not a legal non-compliance determination.

This is where the inquiry takes up a requirement motivated by the earlier inquiry rather than invented here. Preserving the FDP, MapBiomas, and both-source layers as disagreement — instead of averaging them into one number — and refusing to let a screening flag stand in for a legal determination are examples of the documented, uncollapsed disagreement Evidence First, Consensus Later argues a conflict bundle should preserve. That inquiry supplies the epistemic pressure; this one examines how repeated research can turn such pressure into executable method — source separation, temporal-masking rules, validators, screening logic, human-review gates, and agent instructions that keep the layers distinct by construction rather than by reminder. That the resulting code runs deterministically does not, on its own, establish that the layers were drawn correctly; a deterministic procedure and a correct evidence schema are separate properties, and only the second is at stake when the frame itself is wrong.

Source evaluation

The two repositories are strong evidence for some claims and weak evidence for others, and the difference must be kept explicit. The logs record observable state transitions: supersessions, corrections, boundary changes, and the artefacts each round produced. They do not contain the entirety of the human's thought process or the model's internal reasoning. They are records of research-state evolution, not transcripts of cognition.

This limits what the evidence can support. It can support claims about how a method changed and what each change yielded. It cannot support a complete reconstruction of why either party believed what they believed at each step. Any conclusion that leans on the second kind of claim is reading more into the log than the log contains.

The self-referential bundle is unusually candid about this boundary, which strengthens rather than weakens it as evidence. Its metrics table computes the transitions it can measure and marks a whole family of observer-distance measures as not measurable, giving the reason in each case: only one contestation round exists, no timestamped competing predictions were recorded, no independent paired labels over a shared item set were kept. It even excludes one round from its own measurement target to avoid measuring itself in a circle. A record that refuses the claims it cannot support is more trustworthy on the claims it does. The applied bundle shows the same discipline on a different axis: its current answer is marked as requiring human review and its provenance as pinned but not reproduced — the artefacts and hashes were checked, but no qualifying independent rerun was performed — so the reader is told exactly how far the evidence has been verified.

Competing interpretations

Three readings of the recorded trajectory compete, and they are not equally strong. The most serious rival to the claim advanced here is the external-memory interpretation: the log is valuable because it lets a later reader or agent recall earlier states without re-deriving them. This reading is coherent and was the article's own earlier position. Its weakness is that it justifies indefinite retention of everything and gives no account of when history should stop being consulted.

The second reading is narrower still: the log is provenance, a record kept so that a public claim can be traced to its working materials. Conventional dependency-and-provenance tooling already does this well. On this view the trajectory adds auditability but nothing methodological, and the negative answer to the research question holds.

The third reading, which this article tests, treats the log as an intermediate representation — a substrate from which more compact executable knowledge can be compiled. On this view the trajectory's purpose is to be partly consumed: some of it becomes code, some becomes durable instruction, and only the irreducible remainder stays open. The article's evidence bears on whether this third reading is doing real work beyond the first two, or merely renaming them.

Critical synthesis

The recorded trajectories support the weaker, defensible core of the proposition and do not establish its strong general form. What the two cases show is that human–AI inquiry can be progressively reduced into three kinds of output: deterministic procedures where a rule has been found and tested; improved AI instructions and guardrails where judgement remains necessary but a recurring failure has been identified; and explicit unresolved questions where neither reduction is yet justified. The EUDR sequence contributes to this pattern by showing individual review defects becoming more durable method constraints: source separation, temporal-mask rules, report validation, and human-review gates.

Two cautions keep this from becoming a stronger claim than the evidence allows. First, “deterministic” must not be equated with “true.” A Python pipeline can reproducibly implement a wrong conception of the problem; reproducibility preserves whatever it is given, including error. The fabricated-input episode is the standing demonstration: hashes and reruns preserved an invalid evidence state until later inquiry exposed the source-authenticity problem. A reduction can therefore be retired from routine computation only after the reduction itself has survived appropriate scrutiny — and for a public research trail the historical trajectory should normally be archived rather than deleted, even once it has left the AI's operational context.

Second, the evidence is two internal cases, one of them self-referential. That is enough to make the compilation reading plausible and to distinguish it from mere memory or provenance; it is not enough to establish that recorded inquiry generally reduces error or transfers across problems. The honest conclusion is narrow. The trajectory is not necessarily the final knowledge representation. It is an observable substrate through which human–AI research can discover what should become code, what should become instruction, and what cannot yet be reduced. Whether that discovery reliably outperforms deriving the same outputs from the final evidence alone remains open, and the strong claim should not be reported as settled.

Read alongside Evidence First, Consensus Later, the two cases point to two related but distinct levels rather than one. The object level, which that inquiry specifies, runs: environmental claim → evidence → disagreement and contestation → review → governance decision or possible consensus. The method level, examined here, runs: human–AI inquiry → detection of framing, schema, or implementation error → revised evidence architecture → instructions, tests, specifications, or deterministic procedure where the correction has survived enough scrutiny to justify it. The two levels must not be collapsed. Evidence infrastructure constrains what governance can responsibly decide; inquiry and correction constrain what the evidence infrastructure itself is permitted to assume. Put together, the combined architecture is not an irreversible pipeline but an iterative dependency: human–AI inquiry and correction → corrigible evidence architecture → inspectable evidence and disagreement → review and contestation → governance decision or possible consensus. Downstream evidence conflicts can expose defects in the architecture, reopening inquiry and changing rules, schemas, instructions, tests, or deterministic procedures. The EUDR bundle's own current position — human-review-required and pinned-not-reproduced — shows the sequence deliberately stalled at review rather than carried through to a decision.

Implementation path

The proposition implies a concrete discipline: each significant inquiry round should be made to produce its own compression. At the close of a round, the working question is what the change revealed. Did it expose a deterministic rule that belongs in code? A general validation rule that belongs in tests? A recurring reasoning failure that belongs in an AGENTS.md or method-instruction file? A source fact that belongs in the current evidence state? Or an unresolved question that should stay open? Sorting each finding into one of these destinations turns the history into a development substrate rather than an endlessly expanding prompt context.

A sixth destination sits alongside these five: frame or schema correction — a finding that changes what the system treats as a bundle, source, claim, observer, evidence layer, validation boundary, or screening category, rather than a finding within an existing one of those categories. Not every fix deserves that label. A blank image, stale label, or source update may be an ordinary artifact correction; it becomes a frame or schema correction only when it changes the structural categories by which evidence is admitted, separated, or judged. Round four's widening of the self-referential bundle's object of study qualifies. The coffee temporal-mask repair qualifies only in this narrower sense: it changed the relation among baseline coffee, current coffee, and new coffee since the baseline, rather than merely changing a value inside a settled category. Such corrections should not simply disappear into whichever implementation currently supersedes them. Where a frame change materially explains why the current method looks as it does, the fact that the frame changed should remain inspectable in the research trail, even once the obsolete intermediate implementation it produced no longer needs to sit in an AI's active context. This is compatible with, not a reversal of, the earlier point that not every historical step deserves indefinite preservation: what must persist is the record that the frame changed and why, not every artefact the superseded frame produced.

A companion design principle follows for the AI's role. It should not be limited to solving the problem as posed. It should also test whether the posed decomposition is supported by the governing evidence, and whether a more general or more precise formulation is required. The geospatial repository already moves this way: its agent contract requires review of sibling history while prohibiting silent generalisation of dataset-specific thresholds, commodity-specific inference, area-of-interest assumptions, source hierarchies, legal interpretation, or evidence semantics.

Limits

This article does not measure whether compression actually reduces error rates, nor how many historical rounds an AI still needs once good instructions exist, nor whether instructions derived from one trajectory transfer to another. It rests on two cases and cannot generalise from them to a law of method.

It also keeps a deliberate distance from Wolfram's vocabulary. Terms such as computational irreducibility and the bounded observer are useful for naming why a process may resist shortcutting and why a partial observer sees a coarse-grained world. They are used here as vocabulary, not as a demonstrated explanation. In particular, the absence of a discovered deterministic reduction is not evidence of genuine irreducibility; it may only mean the reduction has not been found yet. Deterministic execution and epistemic correctness are separate properties and are treated as such throughout.

Open questions

Can recorded inquiry demonstrably reduce the recurrence of a known class of error, rather than merely document it?

Once good instructions exist, how many historical rounds must still be supplied to an AI for reliable performance?

Do instructions distilled from one trajectory transfer usefully to a different problem, or do they encode context that does not travel?

How can accumulated instructions themselves be detected when they have begun to encode obsolete assumptions?

Can an agent reliably propose that a recurring reasoning operation should now become deterministic code — and be trusted to judge when it should not?
