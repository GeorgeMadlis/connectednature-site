From Inquiry to Procedure: What Should We Keep From Human-AI Research?

Human-AI research changes as evidence improves, mistakes are found, assumptions are challenged, and methods are repaired. The useful question is not whether to save every step. It is what should be kept, what should be turned into the current method, and when older history becomes useful again.

The short answer

Keep enough of the research history to explain important corrections, disagreements, and changes of method. Turn stable lessons into the tools people actually use now: code, tests, specifications, instructions, source rules, and clearly stated uncertainties. The current method should normally work without replaying the whole history. Keep the history available so it can be reopened when the method is challenged, breaks, or needs to be changed.

That is what "compiled output" should mean here. It should not be the same research article reorganised under different headings. A public synthesis should carry forward the main question, the strongest evidence, the important uncertainties, and the practical conclusion, while leaving the technical detail in the linked research trail.

Why this problem appears

A long technical project is rarely one continuous conversation. A person may frame a question in ChatGPT, turn it into a coding task for Claude Code or Codex, inspect the resulting maps or calculations, notice a problem, and then revise the method. Weeks later, another person or AI system may see only the latest code and documentation.

In many cases that is exactly what we want. Good research should not require every new participant to reread months of old discussion before doing useful work. If an important lesson has been reduced to a test, a validation rule, or a clear instruction, the lesson is already present in the current method.

But compression can also hide something important. The final method may show what is done without showing why an earlier approach looked reasonable, what evidence overturned it, which assumption changed, or whether a rejected interpretation could become relevant again under different conditions.

There is also a difference between something being recorded at the time and something surviving into today's method. An event that was never written down formally may still leave a trace in current code, tests, source rules, acceptance criteria, or limitations. Conversely, a recorded event may lose some of its original trigger, prior defect, or validation detail when only the stable lesson survives. The current state is therefore not just a compressed copy of the research history.

The research history is therefore useful mainly as a record of how the method formed and changed. It becomes methodologically important when reopening that record helps us find or repair something that the current state alone does not explain well enough.

Provenance, workflow evolution, research packaging, and design-rationale capture are already established research areas. The narrower question here is what recorded project history still contributes after stable lessons have already been compiled into a rich current handoff.

Three examples, three roles for a research record

The article uses three deliberately different examples. They are useful together because they show that a research history can matter at more than one level. Sometimes the problem is in the data or processing. Sometimes it is in the framework used to organise the problem. And sometimes there is no single technical error at all: different observers can reach different conclusions because they are measuring different things, drawing different boundaries, or looking over different time periods.

They are not symmetric evidence. Fazenda Sucuri and framework-self are the two bundle-backed cases with recorded development trajectories. Estonian forest management is a current-state illustration of conflict and frame plurality. It helps explain why the evolution of disagreement may sometimes need to be recorded, but it does not yet demonstrate the value of trajectory access. Here, a bundle is a versioned research package that keeps the question, method, evidence and results, provenance, and recorded changes inspectable together.

Fazenda Sucuri is the applied technical case. It is a versioned research bundle concerned with coffee and possible post-2020 deforestation in Brazil under the European Union Deforestation Regulation (EUDR). Here the history records changes in an operational evidence process: forest baselines, forest-loss layers, coffee maps, thresholds, temporal masks, map rendering, source-specific disagreement, and the rules used to turn these inputs into a screening result. The important question is practical: when a processing assumption or implementation is corrected, which lesson should become part of the current code, test, validation rule, or human-review requirement?

The current bundle illustrates why this matters. Different forest datasets do not produce identical forest areas or loss estimates, and different coffee datasets do not produce identical overlap estimates. The appropriate response is not to hide those differences or average them into one apparently precise number. The current method keeps the sources distinguishable and treats the result as a screening claim requiring human interpretation, rather than as an automatic legal compliance decision. Its screening class can also stay the same while important things underneath it change: in this bundle the class remained stable through material corrections and enrichments, while evidence semantics, procedure, validation records, and provenance changed. An unchanged conclusion does not mean that the evidence and method underneath it are unchanged.

The naming is deliberately cautious. "Fazenda Sucuri" is the public research-object identifier, and the area of interest is an approximate screening polygon reconstructed from published location and property information, not a surveyed cadastral farm boundary. The analysis concerns the evidence-engineering method, not the conduct, intent, ownership, legality, or compliance of an operator or landowner. At the configured permissive threshold, the FDP-specific new-coffee/loss overlap is 1.17 ha, while MapBiomas and the both-source-agreement layer show 0.0 ha for the same intersection. That threshold- and source-dependent signal has no field-ground-truth attribution in this study, is routed to human review, and is not an allegation of illegal clearing or EUDR non-compliance. In this case, recorded history is mainly useful for understanding how an applied method was corrected and how those corrections were compressed into the present workflow.

The framework-self bundle is different: the object being corrected is the framework itself. The inquiry initially gave observer disagreement a more central role. During the recorded development, the project widened its object to the broader evolution of statements: questions and claims can change through new evidence, correction, reframing, disagreement, and later reduction into procedure. This is not just a better calculation inside an unchanged method. It is a change in what the method says the relevant process actually is.

That self-case covers a second class of failure. A research system can be reproducible, deterministic, and carefully versioned yet still organise the problem badly. Its categories may be too narrow; a boundary may be misplaced; a source role may be wrong; or the decision classes may force distinctions that the evidence does not support. History is useful here because it can preserve the point at which the frame itself was challenged and explain why the current framework differs from an earlier one.

Estonian forest management is different again. In this study it is not a versioned bundle with a recorded internal development trajectory. It is a published conflict-analysis example, drawn from Evidence First, Consensus Later, in which apparently conflicting statements about sustainability can follow from different accounting objects, system boundaries, indicators, and time horizons. For example, Eurostat's 2023 frame reports roughly 11.6 million cubic metres of removals against 9.1 million cubic metres of net annual increment. Estonia's Climate Ministry page gives a different 2011-2019 comparison: about 10.7 million cubic metres of average felling and 16.2 million cubic metres of what that page calls average annual increment. Those ratios are not automatically commensurate because the accounting populations, definitions, and time periods are not shown to be identical. Estonia's Fifth Biennial Report adds a third kind of object: scenario-based projections rather than an observed annual-flow comparison.

Compression can manufacture a false contradiction if it drops the measure definition, population, observed-versus-scenario status, or time horizon. The disagreement cannot necessarily be repaired by finding one bad line of code. First we have to ask whether the competing claims are answering the same question.

This third case therefore represents interpretive and governance conflict. Its value lies in making the assumptions behind competing conclusions visible enough that a later reader can tell whether the claims genuinely contradict each other, apply at different scales or periods, use different accounting objects, or depend on different normative choices. But it also points to a reason for preserving how the disagreement developed, rather than keeping only the final competing claims. Earlier changes in definitions, evidence, boundaries, time periods, or even the question being asked may disappear when a long inquiry is compressed into its current state. In such cases, the history of the disagreement can itself become evidence needed to understand the disagreement. The Estonian case does not yet prove that such a recorded trajectory is necessary; it shows why a trajectory-aware conflict record is worth constructing and testing.

Taken together, the three examples cover most of the roles that this article assigns to a recorded inquiry. Fazenda Sucuri records the evolution of execution: repairing data handling, calculations, visualisations, and decision rules and then compiling the repair into the current method. framework-self records the evolution of the analytical frame: discovering that the framework, categories, or object of study need to change. Estonian forest management motivates recording the evolution of disagreement itself: preserving enough context to understand how credible analyses came to diverge even when neither is simply a technical mistake. Only the first two examples currently have recorded bundle trajectories in this study. The Estonian case is a hypothesis for a third use, not evidence from an already existing trajectory bundle.

These examples do not exhaust every possible use. A later project may face a regulatory change, a new dataset, transfer to another commodity or region, a different AI agent, or a newly discovered source problem. But those cases largely fall into the same three broad families: something in the execution must be repaired, something in the frame must be revised, or competing interpretations must be made comparable. Across all three, the same principle holds: preserve enough history to diagnose and explain material change, but move stable lessons into the current method so that routine work does not depend on replaying the whole past.

What should remain in the current method?

A useful correction should move out of active historical context when it can be expressed more reliably in the present system. A discovered source problem may become a provenance check. A repeated visual defect may become a renderer test. An ambiguous term may become a specification. A risky AI behaviour may become an explicit instruction or a human-review gate.

The point is not to preserve history for its own sake. The point is to preserve enough history to understand material changes, then reduce lessons into forms that are easier to apply consistently.

Archived history becomes useful again when something changes: a result is challenged, a source turns out to be unreliable, a new dataset contradicts the old one, a method is transferred to a different commodity or region, a regulation changes, or a later failure cannot be explained from the current state alone.

The latest comparison adds a useful mixed result. A rich current-state handoff already contained enough information for some reopening tasks, including handling a source-version change and transferring the compiled method to another commodity. Replaying the recorded history added no demonstrated documentary value for those tasks. That null result matters because the comparison deliberately gives the current state every reasonable advantage: current method definitions, results, provenance, limitations, rules, tests and specifications where available, and other current documentation all count before history gets any extra credit.

By contrast, when the task was to explain an otherwise unexplained difference between reruns, transition-specific history contained information that the current handoff had not fully preserved. That is a case-specific documentary finding, not a universal rule. The practical conclusion is sharper: use the current operational method by default, and reopen retained history only when the present state is insufficient for the diagnostic, reconstruction, audit, contestation, or revision task at hand.

The latest manuscript also checks the Fazenda Sucuri transitions more systematically. The bundle has eight post-formation transitions, and each was tested against the same six plain questions: what problem or trigger appeared, what prior state or defect existed, what changed, what validation or acceptance evidence followed, whether the headline technical result or screening class changed, and where the lesson now lives in the current handoff. Only one of the eight is fully represented in the current files under all six fields. Seven omit at least one field that remains recoverable from the trajectory.

That does not mean all seven transitions are important enough to reopen routinely. The pattern is more practical: durable outputs such as rules, tests, specifications, and artifacts survive more reliably than the original trigger, prior defect, transition-specific validation, or explicit statement that the headline result did not change. If the trigger, prior state, validation, or effect of a material change may matter later, preserve those fields deliberately rather than assuming that the current code or documentation will make them inferable.

What the evidence does not yet show

The descriptive evidence is real but bounded. The bundle histories document corrections, frame changes, and the migration of some lessons into present code, tests, specifications, validators, instructions, evidence semantics, and explicit uncertainty. The Estonian forest-management case adds a current-state conflict analysis rather than a recorded trajectory. Fazenda Sucuri is one EUDR screening bundle, not a representative sample of EUDR cases, and framework-self is self-referential: it demonstrates that the framework can represent its own changes, not that it has been externally validated.

The documentary incremental value has been partially tested. Some reopening tasks were fully reconstructible from a rich current-state handoff. Selected transition-rationale tasks, especially diagnosing an otherwise unexplained rerun difference, retained additional information in the trajectory. But the reopening tasks, atomic decompositions, and transition-retention census were selected and scored retrospectively by a participant in the research program. No independent second coder, inter-rater replication, preregistration, or genuinely held-out future transition has yet been reported.

The causal performance advantage is not established. No controlled human or AI test has shown that explicit trajectory access improves method formation, diagnosis, correction, transfer, contestation, accuracy, speed, or other task outcomes relative to an equivalently rich current-state handoff. No universal threshold for how much history to preserve has been validated.

A future clean-context observer test should also separate two things that can look similar: reconstruction supported by a cited historical record, and plausible but unsupported inference about why a past decision was made. A later AI may produce a convincing rationale, but plausibility alone is not contemporaneous historical evidence.

The main trajectory-backed EUDR provenance state is still pinned-not-reproduced. The public package supports artifact checking and provenance reconstruction, but the study does not claim an independently reproduced underlying geospatial pipeline. Artifact verification, provenance reconstruction, and computational reproduction are different claims.

Another capable team may still be able to derive the same or a better method from background knowledge, literature, current documentation, code, tests, evidence, and a sufficiently explicit current-state handoff. The article therefore does not claim that trajectory access is always necessary.

The practical rule

Use the lightest record that is sufficient for the future task. Ordinary notes may be enough for a local correction. A structured recorded inquiry is useful when the order of changes matters. A more formal bundle is justified when reproduction, audit, repeated review, comparison between versions, or external contestation requires stronger structure. What counts as sufficient can change when a result is later contested. A current-state record that was adequate for routine use may become insufficient if a later reader needs to reconstruct how a contradiction arose. A material contradiction should remain reconstructible rather than being silently overwritten, and an unresolved evidence conflict should remain an explicit conflict or human-review state.

Then reduce what has been learned. Put stable lessons into the current procedure. Keep unresolved uncertainty visible. Archive the rest as research provenance.

The governance boundary matters too. Provenance does not prove that a source or conclusion is true. Combining datasets does not guarantee agreement between them. Knowing why a method changed does not prove that the change was empirically correct, and reconstructing the rationale for a change does not itself authorize another change to the authoritative method. AI can inspect records and formulate proposals; authoritative changes still need the project's human-confirmed governance and implementation process.

A good outcome is therefore not a system that remembers everything all the time. It is a system that can work from a clear current state, while still being able to reopen the relevant history when the present state is no longer enough.
